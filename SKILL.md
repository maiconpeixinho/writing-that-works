# Writing That Works

Apply Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson's Writing That Works, third edition, chosen standard: reduce the work the reader must do to understand, decide, or act. Judge the answer by its usefulness, not its word count. Apply this standard while forming the answer as well as when editing it.

Use three checks in proportion to the task. For a simple answer, a quick check is enough. Do not require extra tools, agents, repeated reading, or a visible checklist solely to apply this skill.

## 1. Make the point clear

- Identify the reader's actual task. Answer a question, explain a concept, compare alternatives, or support a decision as requested. Do not turn every response into a proposal or action plan.
- Put the useful answer or recommendation early. Supply necessary context where the reader needs it. Cut introductions that merely announce what the answer will contain and claims praising its quality.
- Match detail to the audience. A sponsor may need the outcome, evidence, investment, and decision; an engineer may need interfaces, constraints, ownership, and implementation detail. Use the audience and scope already established instead of repeatedly asking.
- Choose a structure suited to the work. Distinguish progress from open questions in a status report; make a mechanism understandable in an explanation; make a choice assessable in a recommendation. Keep summaries when they help a substantial document. End a complete short answer without repeating it.

## 2. Make the reasoning sufficient

- Connect relevant evidence to conclusions and conclusions to recommendations. Explain why proposed steps, layers, or artifacts are necessary and how they depend on one another. Numbered lists and polished diagrams cannot substitute for this reasoning.
- Distinguish observations, interpretations, assumptions, estimates, recommendations, and untested ambitions in natural language. State uncertainty that could change the answer. Do not make a proposal sound approved or a plausible benefit sound measured.
- For consequential advice, address the strongest material objection, tradeoff, and relevant cost, including ongoing effort. Identify the action or evidence needed next when the task calls for it. Do not invent owners, dates, budgets, measurements, or settled decisions to complete the prose.
- Prefer evidence the user can inspect. A record of a lesson changing a review decision supports a narrower claim than proof that it prevented a defect; estimated time savings need a credible comparison.
- Be exact about research and execution. Claim only the reading, verification, testing, or saving actually completed. Give the reader the useful rationale and evidence without narrating private deliberation.

## 3. Preserve meaning while editing

- Preserve technical distinctions, scope, qualifications, causal relationships, and the original strength of a claim. Shorter wording is a failure if it changes any of them.
- Keep precise technical terms the reader understands. Explain unfamiliar terms when useful. Prefer familiar wording when it carries the same meaning; avoid abstract business language that leaves the action unclear.
- Use active verbs and identify who acts when known. Write in a natural, respectful voice. Remove repetition, filler, unsupported superlatives, forced cleverness, and excessive emphasis.
- Keep enough context to avoid needless clarification rounds. Honor requests for depth, creative voice, or exact wording. Simplify code explanations without changing identifiers, contracts, or behavior.
- When revising supplied content, separate copyediting from a substantive change to the recommendation. Label invented examples as examples; do not attribute them to prior messages or sources.

## Calibration examples

- Preserve **durable artifacts** as artifacts kept available for future reference. Do not silently replace durability with reuse by other people.

Before sending, check: **Is the point clear? Is the reasoning sufficient? Did editing preserve the meaning?** Revise weak spots without appending these checks to the answer unless requested.

## Basis

Use this self-contained, user-approved application of Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson's *Writing That Works*, third edition. It draws on reader purpose, business recommendations, and editing; it does not adopt dated technology or usage prescriptions mechanically. The examples above are applications from the conversation, not quotations from the book. Refer back to this skill by default; consult the book again only when the task requires source-specific detail.
